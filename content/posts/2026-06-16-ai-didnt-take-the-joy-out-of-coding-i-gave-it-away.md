Earlier this week, I caught myself writing a blog post somewhere between asleep and awake. Not on a screen, but in that half-dream state where your mind drafts things you'd never sit down and type. The thought was, "I don't enjoy being a developer anymore since AI arrived."

I woke up, and the sentence was still there. So I decided to follow it and see where it went.

The first thing I noticed is that the dream had it wrong. "I don't enjoy being a developer anymore" is the easy version, because it blames AI.

Don't get me wrong, I still love developing. That's the part that makes this hard to write.

## Months of evenings

I built Front Matter CMS from nothing. Just an idea, and then a lot of evenings and weekends turning it into something real. Demo Time went the exact same way. When I think about the hours that went into both of those tools, and how an AI workspace agent would have saved me weeks, maybe months, of that work, I keep landing on one question:

> Would they even exist?

Those months weren't wasted. Spending that long on something was how I knew I actually believed in it. If I wasn't willing to give up a year of evenings, it probably wasn't worth building. AI removes that slowness, which means it also removes the filter. I'm not sure yet what that does to which ideas actually get built.

Coding was always a fun hobby for me. The challenges or puzzles were the point. Nobody asked me to do it, I just wanted to build cool things and improve my workflow.

## The five-minute manager

Now I sit on my sofa and tell an agent what to do.

I've made peace with becoming a manager of AI agents. That happens to most of us eventually. When you ask a person to do something, you don't expect them back in five minutes saying it's done and ready for the next task. With an agent, that's exactly what happens. Done. Next. Done. Next.

There's no breathing space, always a next thing to think about. The work used to have a rhythm to it. You'd struggle, you'd get stuck, you'd finally figure it out, and there was this moment of joy when it clicked. Hours in the code, and then done. Figuring it out was the whole reward.

That moment barely happens now, and it's not because I stopped touching the code. I still write a lot of it, but I do way more code reviews. Reviewing is the boring part, which is why I like to use [AI code review tools](https://www.eliostruyf.com/ai-code-review-journey-copilot-coderabbit-macroscope/). Either way, the loop has no friction left in it. And that friction is apparently what I was doing it for in the first place.

{{< blockquote type="note" text="What would have taken months now takes weeks. What would have taken a week takes a day or two. And after those two days, what's next? I'm not sure we're always ready for the answer." >}}

## The puzzles are still there

This part took me a while to admit, because it isn't AI's fault.

The joy always came from solving hard puzzles. Figuring out what's going on, learning a new technique or framework, fighting with something until it finally gave way. Those puzzles still exist! AI didn't make them disappear.

I just hand them over.

I tell the agent to find the issue. Sometimes I guide it a little, give it a hand when it's lost, but that's it. I outsource the hard part, the part I used to love, without thinking, every single time. That's got nothing to do with the AI. That one's on me.

When I ask myself why, the answer comes out sounding completely reasonable: *why put yourself through the hurdle and frustration of finding something, when AI can just figure it out, give you the key points, and you do the fix?*

It's sensible, and it wins every time. It's part of the job now, right?

The same thing happens when I get a new idea. I can spend a week building a prototype, or ask an agent to build one in an afternoon. I keep choosing the faster road, and then wonder why the work feels thinner.

The hurdle and the frustration were the thing that fed me.

{{< blockquote type="important" text="The low-effort path wins in the moment even when the high-effort path is the one I actually wanted." >}}

## Where it went instead

The appetite didn't die. I know this because I can see where it went.

I cycle a lot more now. I enjoy it more, I prioritize it, and the reason is friction I can't outsource. There's a climb, and there's no agent I can prompt to ride it for me. There's a clear finish, and a body-deep *I did that* at the top. It's the feeling coding used to give me, and right now the bike is one of the only places I still go to get it.

The bike is the place where the effort is still mine, and that's what bothers me. It used to be built into the development work, and now it isn't. If I want it, I have to choose it. The question is what's worth choosing it for.

## Looking ahead

Earlier I asked whether Front Matter and Demo Time would exist if I'd had AI when I started them.

They'd exist. They'd exist faster, and maybe they'd even be better. I'm just not sure they'd truly be mine.

I had it wrong earlier. The joy came from the challenges or puzzles, sure, but underneath that I loved building something real, something that made my own work better and that other people use, and knowing the code came from my own hands. The months of evenings were the proof of that.

What AI can quietly take from me is the sense that the thing was mine, and that's where the joy was coming from all along. It hands me the finished thing, the finished thing works, and somewhere in there, I stop being the person who made it and become the person who approved it.

If I had to build Front Matter or Demo Time again from scratch today, all of it by hand, it would take too long. I know I wouldn't do it without AI. I'd reach for it because it helps me immensely. The one thing that would protect the joy is time, and that's what I'm no longer willing to pay.

AI makes me faster. It lets me build things I wouldn't get to otherwise. The time to learn and build is right now, and I'm not giving that up. It takes a feeling with it, the joy, and I'm still figuring out how to keep both.

I don't have a fix at the end of this one, and I might need to write it again in a few months. For now, I know the answer to the question I started with: AI didn't take the joy out of coding, I gave it away.
